## What the site itself uses to qualify

**[VERBATIM]** — homepage, section *"Are we a fit?"*:

> Four questions. About twenty seconds.
>
> **Step 1 of 5: Where are you right now?**
> - Outside Saudi Arabia
> - Inside Saudi Arabia, premium residency
> - Inside Saudi Arabia, standard iqama
> - Saudi or GCC national

**[EXTRAPOLATED]** Three things follow from this being the *first* question.

1. **Residency status, not company size or sector, is the primary axis.** It is what determines whether the MISA route applies at all.
2. **"Outside Saudi Arabia" is listed first** — the intended default reader.
3. **Two of the four answers are effectively disqualifying or redirecting.** A Saudi or GCC national forming a wholly Saudi/GCC-owned company **[SOURCED]** *skips the MISA step entirely* and is not this product. A standard-iqama holder gets the homepage's bluntest answer **[VERBATIM]**: *"A standard iqama on its own does not allow you to own a company."* — followed immediately by *"Your route runs through MISA registration, which is a real path with its own requirements."* The brand qualifies people out, in public, on the homepage.

**[TO CONFIRM: the remaining questions.]** The quiz is labelled "Step 1 of 5" but introduced as "Four questions" — so step 5 is **[EXTRAPOLATED]** most likely the result or email-capture screen rather than a fifth question, leaving **three** questions unseen. Only step 1 was retrievable; the rest render client-side. Those questions are Start Saudi's own qualification logic and would be the single best guide to what a proposal must establish early. See OPEN-QUESTIONS Q27.

## The other axis: activity

**[SOURCED]** Cutting across residency, the activity determines whether the deal is simple, complicated, or impossible:

| Band | Activities | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | Technology, e-commerce, general trading, manufacturing, logistics, tourism, business consulting | 100% foreign ownership is the default |
| **Restricted** | Legal practice, accounting and audit, tax advisory, some engineering and architectural consultancy | Saudi shareholding, commonly cited around 25%, exact figure activity-dependent |
| **Capital-gated** | Retail and wholesale trading at 100% foreign ownership | SAR 30,000,000 capital cited |

**⚠️ Trading appears in two bands, and the sources do not reconcile them.** "General trading" is listed among the activities open to 100% foreign ownership, while "retail and wholesale trading at 100% foreign ownership" carries the SAR 30m capital figure. Both are sourced; neither post defines where the line falls. **[EXTRAPOLATED]** The likely distinction is between trading as an activity classification and *retail/wholesale distribution* specifically, which has long carried its own foreign-ownership conditions — but this is inference. **A trading client must not be classified from this table alone.** See OPEN-QUESTIONS Q26.
| **Closed** | Upstream oil and gas E&P; military equipment manufacturing; certain Hajj and Umrah services within Makkah and Madinah; real estate within the Two Holy Mosques boundaries | Not permitted regardless of structure |

**[EXTRAPOLATED]** For proposal-writing this matters more than segment. **The first analytical act of any proposal is to place the client's activity in one of these four bands and say so plainly** — because the answer changes the ownership structure, the capital requirement, the document list and sometimes the viability of the whole thing. Getting it wrong late is the most expensive mistake available in this market, and getting it right early is the most visible demonstration of *"we confirm yours before you commit"*.

## The four segments

### 1. Foreign SME / owner-operator

**[EXTRAPOLATED]** The core market, the closest fit to the site's voice, and the segment the worked example in `proposal/` is built for.

**Who:** A small overseas business or an individual founder. One or two shareholders, often the founder alone. Turnover measured in single-digit millions or less. Entering Saudi Arabia because a customer, a contract or a visible opportunity pulled them in, not as part of a planned international rollout. **[SOURCED]** ChiefNest (US) is the published example, and Araibia, Moona Executive, Western International, CHSS and Trellis Work read as this shape.

**What they need**
- 100% foreign ownership via MISA — usually available, since these are typically services or tech
- A single-shareholder LLC **[SOURCED]** — "One foreign founder may own 100% alone"
- Remote signing, because travelling is a real cost at this size
- A GM decision: **[VERBATIM — blog]** *"If you own the company, you can list yourself as GM"* — which is usually what they do, and which means they need the GM visa, which means residency
- Total cost visibility before committing — often the binding constraint

**What they worry about**
- *"You don't know what you don't know"* — this segment is the one that line was written for
- **[VERBATIM]** *"I went to a few places first and lost money"* — having already been burned by an adviser
- Whether it will really be fifteen days, and what happens when it is not
- Whether they will be able to pay themselves, hire, and open a bank account — i.e. **operating**, not **registered**
- Capital: whether they must lock up money they do not have

**How to pitch:** The whole path, itemised, with the exclusions named and the attestation burden flagged in week one. Calendar dates, not working-day counts. Price structure stated even where the number is `[TO CONFIRM]`. **[EXTRAPOLATED]** For this segment, honesty about the bank account is worth more than any credential — it is the step most likely to blow their plan, and the one competitors are quietest about.

### 2. Funded startup entering KSA

**[EXTRAPOLATED]** No published example, but the activity profile (tech, e-commerce) is squarely in the open band.

**Who:** Series A–C, regionally expanding, often from the UAE, Europe or the US. Multiple institutional shareholders. A board that has already approved the expansion and a date it was promised for.

**What they need**
- Corporate shareholders, not individuals — which makes the document chain heavier: certificate of incorporation, constitutional documents, board resolution, all attested
- Speed against a funding or launch milestone
- A GM who may be a local hire not yet recruited — a dependency that sits outside registration
- Clarity on whether a **branch** or a **new LLC** fits. **[SOURCED]** A branch **[VERBATIM — blog]** *"can only be licensed to do what your home-country company already does there"*, which constrains a startup that intends to do something new in the market

**What they worry about**
- Missing a board-committed date
- Cap table and shareholder structure surviving the Saudi filing intact
- Whether the entity can hire and pay before the bank account exists

**How to pitch:** **[EXTRAPOLATED]** Lead with the parallelism mechanism and with the critical path, because this buyer thinks in dependencies. Be explicit that the 15 days starts after attestation, and that attestation with institutional shareholders in multiple jurisdictions is the long pole. The registered-vs-operating distinction is the one that saves this deal from a dispute.

### 3. Large corporate entering KSA

**[SOURCED]** Ajlan & Bros Holding, iMENA Holding, OpenSooq, ATAD, Quantum Industrial, FAS Energy, FAS Renewables and the engineering firms suggest Taajeel has real experience here — though under the Taajeel brand, not Start Saudi's.

**Who:** Established international or regional business. Procurement function, legal review, a formal RFP, multiple stakeholders, an existing preferred-supplier process.

**What they need**
- Branch vs subsidiary analysis, done properly and in writing
- Group-level document handling across several jurisdictions
- Governance: who signs, under what authority, and how that is evidenced
- Contractual assurances — SLA, remedy, liability cap, insurance, data protection
- A named account contact and a reporting cadence

**What they worry about**
- Supplier risk, not speed. They are asking whether this firm can be relied on, not whether it is fast
- Regulatory exposure and whether the activity codes were chosen correctly
- Whether the adviser will still exist and still be accountable in three years

**How to pitch:** **[EXTRAPOLATED] This is the segment Start Saudi is currently least equipped to win**, and a proposal writer should know that going in. Almost everything procurement asks for — licence number, insurance, SLA, PDPL posture, tenure, named team — is on the missing list in `03-credentials.md`. The available strengths are Taajeel's volume, the corporate names already on the roster, and the process transparency. The honest move is to lean on Taajeel heavily, and to treat the assurance questions as things to get answered before submitting rather than things to finesse. See OPEN-QUESTIONS Q12, Q14, Q24, Q25.

### 4. Professional services firm

**[SOURCED]** PKF Global, PKF Al Bassam Chartered Accountants, Consolidated Consultants Group, HCP Architecture and Construction, KOVA PMC. **[EXTRAPOLATED]** A meaningful share of the roster, and the hardest cases.

**Who:** Accounting, audit, legal, tax, engineering or architectural practice opening a Saudi arm. Often a partnership rather than a corporate, often part of an international network.

**What they need**
- **Restricted-activity handling.** **[SOURCED]** These are the activities that need Saudi shareholding, commonly cited around 25%
- A Saudi partner, or a structure that satisfies the requirement — and **[TO CONFIRM: whether Taajeel can help identify or structure that relationship](OPEN-QUESTIONS Q28)**, which is a materially different and more sensitive service than filing
- Professional licensing from the relevant regulator, **in addition to** MISA and the CR
- Partner-level qualification recognition and equivalency

**What they worry about**
- Whether they can practise at all, and under whose licence
- Giving up 25% of a practice to a partner they have not met
- Network and brand rules from their international parent
- Professional indemnity and regulatory liability across two jurisdictions

**How to pitch:** **[EXTRAPOLATED]** Slowly and carefully. This is the segment where the standard 15-day promise is least applicable, because the professional licence sits outside it — and saying so up front is both accurate and exactly what the brand does elsewhere. The presence of two PKF entities on the client roster is the strongest single proof point available for this segment. **[TO CONFIRM: whether the 15-day promise is offered at all for restricted activities.]** See OPEN-QUESTIONS Q29.

## Who is not a fit

**[SOURCED]**, stated or clearly implied by the site:

- **Saudi or GCC nationals** forming a wholly Saudi/GCC-owned company — no MISA step, so not this product
- **Standard iqama holders** expecting to own a company on that basis — redirected, on the homepage, to the MISA route
- **Anyone in a closed activity** — upstream oil and gas E&P, military equipment manufacture, restricted Hajj/Umrah services, real estate within the Two Holy Mosques boundaries

**[EXTRAPOLATED]** The brand disqualifies openly and early. A proposal should be willing to do the same — an early, clearly-reasoned "this structure will not work, here is what will" is more consistent with this voice than a proposal that takes the brief as given and discovers the problem at filing.

## Cross-segment: what every client needs to be told early

**[EXTRAPOLATED]** Regardless of segment, four things are true, unwelcome and better said in the proposal than discovered later:

1. **Attestation comes first and it is yours.** The 15-day clock starts after it.
2. **Registered is not operating.** The CR does not mean you can trade.
3. **The bank account is the real bottleneck** — 2–3 weeks after CR, run by the bank, outside anyone's control.
4. **Your activity code determines your ownership, your capital and your approvals.** Settle it before anything else.
