# Document preparation, Power of Attorney and attestation

**Sits before the 15-working-day clock. Largely the client's responsibility. Named in the sources as the single biggest cause of delay.**

This is placed first, before the MISA licence, because that is the order it happens in — and because a proposal that leads with the MISA licence has already misled the client about where their time will go.

---

## What it is

**[SOURCED]** Before any filing can be made, a foreign founder or foreign parent company must produce a set of documents that Saudi authorities will accept: legalised, translated into Arabic, and consistent with each other. For a founder outside the Kingdom this means a **Power of Attorney** plus an **attestation chain** on every corporate document.

**[VERBATIM — blog]** A Power of Attorney is *"a signed document in which you, as the founder or the signatory for a parent company, name a specific person to act for you on specific matters."*

The critical property **[SOURCED]**: it must be **specific, not general**. It must identify *"the exact acts your representative may perform"* — the MISA investment licence application, the Ministry of Commerce registration, the commercial registration, the signing of the articles of association, and related filings. A general POA will be rejected.

## What the client provides

**[SOURCED]** For an **individual founder**: passport copies, the executed Power of Attorney, and personal documentation as required by the activity.

**[SOURCED]** For a **corporate shareholder or parent** (from the branch post, and applicable wherever a company is a shareholder):

- Certificate of incorporation
- Memorandum and articles of association
- Certificate of good standing
- Board resolution approving the Saudi entity or branch
- Power of attorney
- Recent audited financial statements

All **authenticated and translated into Arabic**.

### The list as Taajeel states it to a client

**[SOURCED — Taajeel's Havenstone proposal, June 2026.]** For a foreign-owned LLC under a MISA services licence, Taajeel's own client-facing document list is:

- **Articles of Association** — *"should not be less than one year old, and the company must be registered outside of KSA"*
- **Trade Licence** for the company
- **Financial statements of the last year**
- **Power of Attorney** issued by a certified public notary in the legal entity's country

> *"All these documents must be certified from The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and then the Saudi Embassy in the country where the client's legal entity is registered."*

**[EXTRAPOLATED]** Two things worth noticing. The AoA age requirement means a **newly incorporated** foreign parent cannot immediately open a Saudi subsidiary — a constraint that appears nowhere on the Start Saudi site and that would blindside a founder who has just incorporated at home. And the "last year" financial statements means the **most recently filed** accounts; a client mid-way through an audit of the current year is not held up by it.

The same proposal states, for activities under a services licence: *"There are no minimum capital requirements"* and *"There is no minimum commitment to investing requirement during the first five years."*

## The two attestation routes

**[SOURCED]**

**Hague Apostille Convention members** — documents **[VERBATIM — blog]** *"can be apostilled in your home country and then submitted for attestation by Saudi authorities, without a separate Saudi embassy visit."*

**Non-member countries** — the full chain:
1. Notarisation in the home country
2. Attestation by the home Ministry of Foreign Affairs
3. Attestation by the Saudi embassy in that country
4. Attestation by the Saudi Ministry of Foreign Affairs

**[EXTRAPOLATED]** Establishing which route applies is a two-minute question with weeks of consequence, and it belongs on the first page of any proposal where the client's jurisdiction is known.

## What it depends on

- The client's home jurisdiction and its Hague status
- Whether shareholders are individuals or companies — corporate shareholders multiply the documents
- How many jurisdictions the shareholders sit across
- Whether the parent's documents are current (a certificate of good standing has a shelf life)
- Availability of certified Arabic translation

## What commonly goes wrong

**[SOURCED]** — the blog lists these directly:

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **A general POA** lacking specific authorised acts | Rejected; the whole chain restarts |
| **Incomplete attestation chain** — a step skipped | Rejected at filing |
| **Missing certified Arabic translations** | Filing cannot proceed |
| **Name or date mismatches across documents** | **[SOURCED]** sends steps *"back into queues"* |
| **Incorrectly assuming apostille eligibility** | Weeks lost discovering the embassy chain was required |

**[VERBATIM — blog]**, on the biggest single cause of delay for foreign applicants:

> "Document readiness before submission. Attestation, notarisation and certified translation of shareholder and corporate documents happen before the 15-working-day clock starts, and how quickly you can source them is usually the biggest variable in how soon you finish."

And **[VERBATIM — blog]**, on what the client can do about it:

> "Since the 15-working-day timeline assumes your documents are already complete, preparing your paperwork before you begin is the most direct way to bring your overall timeline forward."
